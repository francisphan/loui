The Lighthouse Keepers of Ushant

By Marguerite Penhoat

For more than a century the lights of Ushant were kept by families who lived beside them, climbing the spiral stairs at dusk to trim the wicks and wind the clockwork that turned the great lenses. The island sits at the mouth of the English Channel, where the Atlantic swell meets the tide, and its reefs have claimed more ships than almost any coast in Europe.

The keepers wrote everything down. Their logbooks record the weather at every watch, the ships that passed, the fog signals sounded and the oil burned. Read today, they are a quiet record of patience: page after page of wind directions and visibility, broken now and then by a wreck, a rescue, or a storm that shook the tower until the glass rang.

A day in the tower

A keeper's day began before sunset. The lamp had to be lit at the exact minute the tables gave, and the light could not fail until sunrise. Between watches there was painting, polishing of the brass, and endless cleaning of the lens, because a film of salt or soot cut the beam's reach by miles.

The work was listed in a set order that every keeper learned by heart:

  • Trim the wicks and fill the reservoir
  • Wind the clockwork every two hours
  • Sound the fog signal when the far buoy is lost from sight

Further reading is in the keepers' own accounts, and a bad link that should lose its address. The last keeper left Ushant in 1990, when the lights were automated; the logbooks went to the archive at Brest, where they can still be read by anyone who asks.

Those who remember the keepers speak of them with a particular respect. They were not heroes in the usual sense, but people who did the same small things correctly every night for decades, knowing that one missed night could cost a ship and everyone on it.
